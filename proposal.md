# Preparing Australia's Workforce for the Generative AI Transition

**Policy client:** Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)

**Policy question:** How should DEWR support workers and employers to adjust to AI-related task change while preserving productivity-enhancing AI adoption?

## Executive Summary
Generative AI is spreading across Australian businesses, but its labour-market effects remain early and uneven. In 2024–25, 12% of Australian businesses reported using AI, compared with 1% in 2022–23 (ABS 2026). DEWR finds no evidence to date of broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval, although occupations with greater potential exposure to automation have experienced slower employment growth; the department describes this evidence as suggestive rather than causal (DEWR 2026). Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) also finds that augmentation currently outweighs automation and that outcomes depend on how firms adopt and adapt to AI (JSA 2025).

This proposal evaluates three labour-market adjustment mechanisms and adopts an **AI Workforce Redeployment Partnership** as its recommended package. The proposed two-year pilot would co-fund employer-led retraining and redeployment for up to 10,000 workers whose tasks are materially changed by AI, at an illustrative public cost of A$44 million.
## Problem Definition & Context
Australia faces an adjustment challenge rather than an established AI-driven unemployment crisis. AI adoption is increasing rapidly but unevenly across industries. ABS data show that AI use reached 38% of businesses in Information Media and Telecommunications and 24% in both Professional, Scientific and Technical Services and Financial and Insurance Services in 2024–25, compared with 6% in Construction (ABS 2026). However, exposure to AI should not be treated as equivalent to job loss. JSA's task-level analysis finds that current generative AI has greater potential to augment workers than automate them overall, with automation potential concentrated more heavily in routine tasks (JSA 2025).

This distinction matters for policy design. Australia's National AI Plan already supports broad AI skills development and recognises that AI may reshape tasks rather than eliminate entire occupations, making reskilling, career support and workforce mobility important (DISR 2025). The remaining policy problem is therefore not simply a lack of general AI literacy. It is whether workers and employers can adjust quickly enough when particular tasks are automated or redesigned. Credit constraints, training costs and employer underinvestment in portable skills may slow adjustment, while the costs of technological change may fall disproportionately on affected workers even when AI raises overall productivity.
## Analysis of Labour Market Impacts
A task-based framework provides a better explanation of AI's labour-market effects than treating entire occupations as either exposed or protected. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) distinguish a **displacement effect**, where technology allows capital to perform tasks previously undertaken by labour, from a **reinstatement effect**, where new tasks create areas in which labour retains a comparative advantage. This distinction is particularly relevant to generative AI. JSA (2025) finds that augmentation currently outweighs automation across the Australian labour market, while automation potential is more concentrated in routine tasks. Employment effects therefore depend on which tasks change and whether firms redesign jobs around remaining and newly created human tasks.

Evidence also shows that AI can complement workers and raise productivity. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) studied 5,179 customer-support agents and found that access to a generative AI assistant increased productivity by approximately 14% on average. Gains were largest for novice and lower-skilled workers, whose productivity increased by around 34%. This suggests AI can transfer knowledge and narrow productivity differences between workers rather than simply replacing labour.

However, these gains are highly task-dependent. Dell'Acqua et al. (2026) found that knowledge workers using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks and worked 25.1% faster on tasks within AI's capability frontier. On a task outside that frontier, AI users were 19% less likely to reach the correct solution. Effective adoption therefore requires complementary human skills such as judgement, verification and recognising when AI should not be relied upon.

## Policy Options
Three policy options address different barriers to labour-market adjustment and can be compared against economic efficiency, equity, targeting, administrative feasibility and fiscal cost.

| Option | Mechanism | Key trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Individual AI Transition Accounts** | Provide eligible workers with up to A$5,000 for approved retraining into new or AI-complementary tasks. A 10,000-worker pilot plus administration would have an illustrative public cost of A$55 million. | Worker-controlled and portable, which may improve occupational mobility. However, subsidies may fund training workers would have undertaken anyway, and course-fee support does not address income lost while studying. |
| **2. Employer AI Redeployment Partnership** | Co-fund employers up to A$4,000 per worker to retrain and redeploy employees whose tasks are materially changed by AI. A 10,000-worker pilot plus administration would have an illustrative maximum public cost of A$44 million. | Preserves worker–firm matches and intervenes before redundancy, while employer co-funding shares costs. However, firms may seek subsidies for training they would already have provided, requiring verification of AI-related task change and redeployment. |
| **3. AI Transition Training Allowance** | Replace 50% of previous earnings, capped at A$800 per week for up to 12 weeks, for workers undertaking approved retraining after AI-related displacement. An illustrative 5,000-worker pilot would cost about A$46 million. | Addresses liquidity constraints and improves access for workers unable to forgo earnings. However, it has a higher cost per participant and requires government to establish whether income or hours reductions are genuinely linked to AI. |

International experience demonstrates similar mechanisms. Singapore combines individual training credits with income support for eligible mid-career training, while Germany's Qualifizierungsgeld provides income replacement during employer-linked retraining for workers affected by structural change (SkillsFuture Singapore 2026; Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2026). These schemes illustrate different ways of sharing adjustment costs rather than providing direct templates for Australia.

The options therefore solve different problems: Option 1 reduces the price of acquiring skills, Option 2 supports job redesign before displacement occurs, and Option 3 reduces the opportunity cost of retraining. Their relative suitability depends on whether the policy objective prioritises worker portability, employment retention or income protection.
## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan
The selected package is a two-year **AI Workforce Redeployment Partnership** administered by DEWR with analytical support from Jobs and Skills Australia. The pilot would cover up to 10,000 workers and provide matched government funding of up to A$4,000 per worker for approved retraining and redeployment, producing a maximum training subsidy of A$40 million. An additional A$4 million would be allocated to administration and evaluation, giving an illustrative total public cost of **A$44 million over two years**.

Employers would only qualify where they can demonstrate that AI adoption has materially changed workers' tasks, submit a retraining and redeployment plan, make a financial contribution and commit to retaining participating workers for a minimum period. Training would need to support redesigned, transferable or AI-complementary skills rather than general AI literacy.

Implementation would occur in four stages. During months 1–3, DEWR and JSA would establish eligibility rules, approved training criteria and baseline indicators. Employer applications would open in months 4–6, followed by training and redeployment during months 7–18. Months 19–24 would be used for independent evaluation before a decision on continuation, modification or expansion.

Performance should be measured through training completion, 6- and 12-month employment retention, redeployment rates, changes in earnings, employer co-contributions, cost per successfully redeployed worker and **additionality**—whether public funding generated retraining that would not otherwise have occurred.
## Risks & Mitigations
The pilot faces several implementation risks. First, **deadweight spending** may arise if firms receive subsidies for training they would have funded anyway. Employer co-contributions, evidence of material AI-related task change and an additionality test should reduce this risk.

Second, employers may classify ordinary restructuring as AI-related to access funding. DEWR should therefore require documented changes to workers' task bundles and retain audit powers over participating firms.

Third, retraining may not prevent later redundancy. Funding should therefore be conditional on a minimum post-training retention commitment and on training being linked to redesigned or transferable skills rather than narrow firm-specific tasks.

The scheme may also distribute benefits unevenly because casual workers, freelancers and people already unemployed are less likely to be covered by employer-led redeployment. This limitation should be explicitly monitored during the pilot, with the final evaluation examining whether a separate worker-directed pathway is required.

Finally, AI capabilities and workplace applications are likely to change during implementation. Eligibility rules and approved training categories should therefore be reviewed throughout the pilot, with a formal evaluation at the end of year two before any continuation or expansion.
